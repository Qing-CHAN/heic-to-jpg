HEIC vs JPG: What's the Difference?

A plain-English guide for iPhone photographers · 4 min read

If you have ever plugged an iPhone into a Windows PC and found your photos won't open, you have already met the HEIC vs JPG problem. Both are photo formats; they differ in how they squeeze images down and who can read them. Here is the whole story in plain language.

The short version

HEIC (High Efficiency Image Container) is Apple's default since iOS 11. It stores photos at roughly half the file size of JPG with equal or better quality, and supports extras like burst frames and depth data. The catch: outside Apple's ecosystem, support is patchy.

JPG is the 30-year-old universal standard. Nearly every device, browser, website and printer on earth can open it. It compresses photos by discarding detail your eye barely notices, which is why files are bigger at the same visual quality.

Side-by-side comparison

File size: HEIC wins — a 3MB JPG photo is typically 1.5MB as HEIC with no visible difference.

Compatibility: JPG wins — HEIC needs modern Windows 10/11 (with extensions), current browsers, or a converter. JPG opens everywhere.

Editing support: JPG wins — older versions of Photoshop, Lightroom and many online tools still reject HEIC.

Quality per byte: HEIC wins — less compression artefacting at the same file size.

Features: HEIC wins — stores live photos, bursts, 16-bit colour and transparency.

When should you convert HEIC to JPG?

Keep your iPhone shooting in HEIC — it saves space and Apple photos apps handle it natively. Convert to JPG at the moment of sharing, specifically when:

  • Uploading to websites, government portals or school systems that reject HEIC
  • Sending photos to someone with an older Android or Windows device
  • Submitting documents where the system says "JPG or PNG only"
  • Using older photo editors or print shops

Can you just switch the iPhone to JPG?

Yes — go to Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible and new photos will be saved as JPG. But you lose the storage savings of HEIC, and existing photos stay in HEIC. The bottom line

HEIC is a better storage format; JPG is a better sharing format. Keep HEIC on your phone, convert to JPG (or PNG) when a device or website can't read it — and use a converter that respects your privacy by doing the work locally.
