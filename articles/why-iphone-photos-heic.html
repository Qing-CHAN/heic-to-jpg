Why Are My iPhone Photos HEIC?

The reason behind the format, and what to do about it · 4 min read

You connected your iPhone to a PC, opened the photos folder, and found files ending in .heic instead of .jpg. Nothing is wrong with your phone — Apple made a deliberate choice in 2017, and here's the logic behind it.

The storage math

Photos are the biggest thing on most phones. In 2017, with iPhone storage maxing out and 4K video arriving, Apple adopted HEIC (High Efficiency Image Container), based on the HEIF standard. The headline number: the same visual quality at roughly half the file size of JPG. On a 256GB phone holding tens of thousands of photos, that difference amounts to tens of gigabytes — years of extra headroom.

What HEIC packs inside

  • Higher-quality compression (fewer blocky artefacts than JPG at the same size)
  • 16-bit color support instead of JPG's 8-bit
  • One container for Live Photo frames, bursts and depth maps
  • Transparency support, like PNG

The compatibility hangover

The catch is the rest of the world. Windows handles HEIC with extra extensions, older Android phones often not at all, and countless websites, government portals, school systems and print shops still demand "JPG or PNG only". That mismatch is why you end up searching for a converter in the first place — and why free local converters like this one exist.

Your three options

  1. Keep HEIC, convert on demand — the default and our recommendation. Photos stay compact; when a website or device rejects one, run it through a browser converter in seconds.
  2. Transfer differently — with Settings → Photos → Transfer to Mac or PC → Automatic, cable transfers deliver JPG copies automatically.
  3. Shoot JPG permanently — Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible. Simple, but you pay with double storage. Details in the camera format guide.

What about HEIF 2 — and why converters keep breaking

The HEIC story isn't frozen. Recent iPhones ship photos with additional layers — gain maps for HDR brightness, tile-based encoding for faster previews — that older conversion engines (many online tools still run code from 2019) simply can't parse. If a converter worked last year and chokes on your new photos, that's usually why: the tool is stale, your phone isn't.

The industry is also moving toward HEIF 2, an updated version of the container with better compression again. Apple has committed to it in recent hardware, which means the "my converter broke" problem will keep happening to tools that don't maintain their engines. When choosing a converter, look for one that names its engine version or mentions support for recent iPhone models — it's the difference between a tool that works and one that worked.

Bottom line

HEIC is not a problem — it's a space-saving upgrade that occasionally needs a translator. Keep it as your default, and keep a one-click converter bookmarked for the day a form says "JPG only".
