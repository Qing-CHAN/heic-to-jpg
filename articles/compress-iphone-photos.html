How to Compress iPhone Photos (Without Ruining Them)

For email limits, upload portals and full storage warnings · 5 min read

"File too large." Three words that ruin the moment — whether you're emailing photos to a print shop, uploading to a portal with a 5MB limit, or just watching your storage bar turn red. Here's how to shrink iPhone photos the smart way, from zero-effort to fine-grained.

First: HEIC is already doing you a favor

If your photos are still in HEIC (the iPhone default), they are already about half the size of the JPG equivalent at the same quality. So before compressing anything, check what you're actually working with — often the winning move is simply keeping HEIC where possible and converting only what needs converting. (Full story in why iPhone photos are HEIC.)

Method 1: Convert to JPG at 92% quality (the sweet spot)

Converting a HEIC photo to JPG at a high quality setting typically produces a file that's still perfectly shareable while landing under most upload limits. Our free converter uses 92% quality — visually indistinguishable from the original in everyday viewing, and each converted file shows you the before/after size right in the list, so you know exactly what you got.

Method 2: Let the platform compress for you

  • WhatsApp / Telegram: sending through them compresses to a fraction of the size — fine for viewing, not for printing
  • Email "large version": Mail on iOS offers to reduce size automatically when photos push past the 20MB limit

Fastest routes, zero quality control — fine for casual sharing.

Method 3: For the web — convert to WebP

If the destination is a website, blog or online shop, WebP typically beats JPG by another 25–35% at the same visual quality. The same converter offers WebP output — switch the format to WebP and convert as usual. (One caveat: Safari's image tools still can't encode WebP, so on an iPhone or Mac you'll get a PNG fallback — do it in Chrome or Edge instead.)

Method 4: Bundle for paperwork — PDF

For document workflows (scanned receipts, IDs, forms), a multi-page HEIC-to-PDF is often the answer: one tidy file instead of a photo pile, and PDF portals accept it everywhere.

The golden rule

Compress for the destination, not for everything. Keep the HEIC originals on your phone; make the small, throwaway versions on demand. Storage stays low, quality stays high, and you never permanently damage a photo for the sake of one upload form.
